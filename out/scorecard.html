Our results, in public

How accurate have we actually been?

Every projection here was written down before the deadline it applies to, and locked so it cannot be quietly edited later. This page shows how each one actually did — including the weeks we lost.

Anyone in fantasy football can tell you their tips are good. This is the page that proves it either way, and it is the reason the whole thing exists.

What we are committing to, in advance

Before every deadline we publish a projection for every player. Then we mark it against the same dull benchmark every single week: just assume each player repeats his average from the last six weeks. It sounds too simple to be hard, and it is not — most tipsters would lose to it.

We check ourselves against an even easier benchmark too (a player’s season-long average), but we lead with the harder one, because beating only the easy one would not be worth claiming. Weeks we lose appear in this table looking exactly like the weeks we win.

Each row also carries a fingerprint (a SHA-256) of the exact data file the projection was built from, so anyone can rerun it and confirm we did not change our answer after the results came in.

Our score measures how well we put the players in the right ORDER, not whether we hit any single number. 1.00 would be a perfect ranking, 0.00 no better than shuffling them. The simple guess is what you get by assuming every player repeats his average of the last six weeks — a deliberately dull benchmark that is surprisingly hard to beat, which is exactly why we use it. (For the statistically minded: it is Spearman rank correlation across the eligible player pool.) A week stays pending until its results are final. Locked in is how long before the deadline the scored projection was written; any earlier ones on record are noted beside it. We publish from three days out and re-project as team news lands — the scored entry is always the last one before the deadline, and the earlier ones stay on the record so you can see how much the projection improves as news arrives.

Check this yourself. Each row’s snapshot column is the SHA-256 of the API response the projection was built from, recorded before the deadline. Running verify.py re-derives every published projection from its own snapshot and confirms the output is identical; it reports fail if an input was altered, and drift if the model has been refitted since. We do not ask to be taken on trust.

What we knew before launch

Why our numbers look smaller than everyone else’s

Other sites will show you a forward on 6.6 and a midfielder on 7.1 and a squad predicted to score 75. Ours says 4.9 for the same forward. One of us is wrong, and it is checkable: across every projection we made for the whole of 2025-26 — 8,340 player-gameweeks — here is what reality paid out when we said a number.

When we said six points, reality paid about ten percent less. Our biggest numbers are, if anything, a touch generous — the opposite of too small. And for scale: across all of 2025-26, exactly one player in the league sustained 6.3 points a week, and only four sustained 5.0. A prediction that needs half a squad performing at that level is not a projection, it is a compliment.

Known limitations

  • Opening gameweeks are the weakest. Before the season has data, projections lean on last season and on price. Expect the largest errors in August.
  • The bonus-point term is stale. FPL re-specified BPS for 2026/27; our term was fitted on the previous formula, so defender bonus is likely over-projected until it is refit on this season’s data.
  • No news ingestion yet. Injury and rotation news reaches the model only through FPL’s own availability flags.
  • Single-gameweek football is mostly noise. Even a perfect model would correlate only modestly with one week’s actual points. Judge any projection service over a season, not over a gameweek — including this one.
