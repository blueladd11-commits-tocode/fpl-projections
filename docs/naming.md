# Naming

Research date: 2026-08-20. All domain, handle and registrar observations below were
made on that date and decay fast — re-check before you buy.

## The strategic question

Every product in this category is named after *what it is* ("FPL Review", "Fantasy
Football Hub"); this one's only durable asset is *that it keeps receipts* — so does
the name describe the category and disappear into it, or describe the receipts and
pay an explanation tax?

---

## 1. The landscape, and the convention everyone follows

Observed names in the category:

| paid incumbents | free / freemium tools |
|---|---|
| Fantasy Football Scout (~£30/yr, owned by Enthusiast Gaming) | LiveFPL |
| Fantasy Football Hub (~£40/yr) | FPL Form |
| Fantasy Football Fix | FPL Review |
| Fantasy Football Pundit | FPL Team, FPL Copilot, FPL Optimized, FPL Pulse |
| | FPL Dashboard (fpl.page), Premier Fantasy Tools, Planet FPL |

The convention is rigid and has exactly one shape:

**`[FPL | Fantasy Football] + [common English noun]`**

Scout. Hub. Fix. Form. Review. Team. Pundit. Pulse. Copilot. Dashboard. Tools.
Not one of them is a brand. All of them are *category descriptions with a
qualifier*, and the qualifier is doing all the differentiating work.

Three consequences worth being blunt about:

1. **The names are interchangeable.** "FPL Review" and "FPL Form" and "FPL Team"
   are the same name with the noun swapped. Nobody in this market has spent a
   penny building a name, because the names are all placeholders.
2. **None of them are defensible marks.** "FPL" + a descriptive noun is close to
   unregistrable in Class 41/42 without acquired distinctiveness. Fantasy Football
   Scout has fifteen years of use and could probably argue it; a new entrant
   cannot. If you pick "FPL Projections", anyone can launch "The FPL Projections"
   next August and you have no route to stop them.
3. **The SEO logic that produced the convention is dying.** These names were built
   for a world where `fplreview.com` ranked for "fpl review". Discovery in this
   category now runs through podcasts, YouTube, X and r/FantasyPL — spoken and
   typed word-of-mouth, where a distinctive name outperforms a keyword-stuffed one.

### The trade-off, taken seriously

**Sit inside it.** You are instantly legible. A stranger seeing "FPL Scorecard" in a
tweet knows within one second what it is and that it is not the official game. Zero
explanation cost, at a moment when the product has no brand equity and cannot
afford any. Cost: you are invisible in a field of eleven identical names, you own
nothing legally, and you have permanently capped how large the thing can get —
"FPL Scorecard" cannot become a Serie A product, a Champions League product, or a
general forecasting brand without a rename.

**Break it.** You get a name people can actually repeat, an asset you can register,
and room to expand. Cost: the first two seasons every mention needs a five-word
appositive ("Proper Score, the FPL projections site"), and you are spending
attention on a name at a moment when you should be spending it on the accuracy
record.

**The resolution is not a compromise between the two — it is a split.** Take a
distinctive name for the *brand* and a boringly descriptive line for the *product*,
locked together everywhere: **`Proper Score — FPL projections, on the record`**.
The distinctive half is what a podcast says; the descriptive half is what Google
indexes and what a stranger reads. Descriptive names cannot be un-genericised
later, but a distinctive name can shed its descriptor once it is known. The split
is strictly one-way, so take the option that keeps the door open.

One further argument for breaking convention that is specific to this product:
**the positioning is an accusation.** "Every competitor asserts, we prove" only
lands if you don't look like every competitor. A name that sits in the middle of
the FPL-prefix pile visually agrees with the pile.

## 2. Trademark and collision risk

**The hard line.** The Football Association Premier League Ltd holds "PREMIER
LEAGUE" registrations in the UK and EU dating to 1992/1998, including Class 41
("services relating to sports' events and matches; all relating to the promotion of
Association football") — which is the class this product sells into. The Premier
League's own terms state that the marks on its site "are owned by us or our
suppliers, partners or member Clubs" and that "no rights are granted to use any of
them without the prior written permission of the owner." Its IP Enforcement Policy
runs an active programme with outside counsel (DLA Piper UK LLP) and investigators,
empowered to bring "civil or criminal legal proceedings".

So:

- **Never put "Premier League" in the name.** Not "Premier League Projections", not
  "PL xP", not "Fantasy Premier League Scorecard". For a *paid* product in Class 41
  this is not a grey area — it is both direct infringement exposure and an implied-
  endorsement problem, and the paid subscription removes every fair-use argument
  you might have had as a hobby site.
- **Avoid anything that implies officialdom.** "Official", "The Scout" (the Premier
  League runs its own tips column at `fantasy.premierleague.com/the-scout` —
  Fantasy Football Scout lives with a genuine confusion problem you should not
  volunteer for), purple in the vicinity of PL purple, or the PL lion in any form.

**The strongest available evidence on where the line actually sits is what the
incumbents did.** Not one commercial operator in the category uses "Premier League"
in its name — including Fantasy Football Scout, which is owned by a publicly listed
company (Enthusiast Gaming) with real lawyers and every incentive to grab the
keyword if it were safe. They all chose "Fantasy Football" or "FPL" instead. That
is a whole market's independent legal assessment, and it agrees.

**Is "FPL" safe by convention, or crowded to uselessness?**

Both, and they are separate questions with separate answers.

- **Legally: safe by convention, with a disclaimer.** No dedicated "FPL" wordmark
  registration turned up for the Football Association Premier League Ltd in
  searching (an inconclusive result — I could not reach the UK IPO or EUIPO
  registers directly from this environment; trademarkia.com returned HTTP 403.
  **Get a UK trade mark attorney to run a proper clearance search before you file
  anything.**) The practical evidence is strong: at least ten commercial FPL tools,
  several of them paid, have used the "FPL" prefix for years without being forced
  to rebrand. The pattern they use is FPL-prefix *plus* an explicit non-affiliation
  notice — the App Store listing for "Fantasy Football: FPL Scout" carries exactly
  this: "an independent app and is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or associated
  with the Premier League or Fantasy Premier League." Whatever you pick, carry that
  line in the footer and the app store copy. It is free and it removes the
  implied-endorsement argument.
- **Commercially: crowded to uselessness.** "FPL" as a prefix buys you nothing.
  Eleven products already have it, it is a generic abbreviation you cannot own, and
  it makes your name a template rather than a name. It is safe in the way that
  wearing the same shirt as everyone else is safe.

**Two candidate-specific collisions to note:** "Ledger" is a famous mark (Ledger
SAS, hardware wallets) in classes 9/36 — a football statistics service in 41/42 is a
different field, but the fame of the mark plus your "cryptographically snapshotted"
language makes the crypto adjacency real rather than theoretical. And "FPL
Receipts" is not a trademark question at all; the name is simply already in use
(below).

## 3. How the evidence below was gathered

- **`.com` / `.co.uk` / `.io`**: `whois` against the authoritative registry servers
  (`whois.verisign-grs.com`, `whois.nic.uk`, `whois.nic.io`), plus `dig +short NS`.
- **`.app`**: registry whois was unusable, so RDAP via `rdap.org`, validated with
  controls — `google.app` → 200 (taken), a nonsense string → 404 (free).
- **X**: HTTP status and `<title>` of `x.com/<handle>`. Controls:
  `@zqxjkwvbnmqp7788` → 404 "User Profile Not Found"; `@FantasyPL` → 200 with a real
  profile title. A 404 means no profile exists; it does **not** guarantee X will let
  you register the string (reserved and suspended handles also 404). Confirm at
  signup.
- **Reddit: inconclusive, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.** Every request
  to reddit.com returned HTTP 403 from this environment, including the control
  `r/FantasyPL` — a subreddit that definitely exists. No candidate name surfaced in
  web-indexed Reddit content, which is weak negative evidence only. **Check
  subreddit and username availability by hand before committing.**

## 4. Shortlist

### 1. Proper Score

**Means.** A *proper scoring rule* is one where a forecaster maximises their
expected score only by reporting what they actually believe — the formal definition
of a scorecard you cannot game by lying. In British English, "proper" also reads as
genuine, real, the actual thing.

**Promises.** Our numbers are graded by a rule that punishes us for bluffing, and we
publish the grade. Reads as confident-without-hype to a layman and as a precise
technical claim to the ~5% of the audience who work with forecasts.

**Evidence.** `properscore.com` **TAKEN** — created 2015-09-12, registrar
Cloudflare, expiry 2027-04-17, serves no page content (dormant/held, not an active
business; a purchase approach is plausible but unpriced). `properscore.co.uk`
**FREE**. `properscore.io` **FREE**. `properscore.app` **FREE**. X `@properscore`
**free** (404), `@theproperscore` **free**. Reddit inconclusive.

**Strongest argument against.** The technical claim is slightly wrong, and the
people most likely to be impressed by it are the people most likely to catch it:
your headline metric is Spearman, which is *not* a proper scoring rule at all
(RMSE and Brier are). A product whose entire pitch is rigour should not have a
name a statistician can nitpick in one sentence. Secondary: "proper" is UK-coded
and reads flat or slightly odd to the large non-British share of FPL's audience,
and losing the `.com` to a dormant holder means a `.co.uk`-primary business with a
permanent acquisition negotiation hanging over it.

### 2. FPL Scorecard

**Means.** Exactly what it says. The scorecard is already the product's internal
name for the differentiating artifact (`publish.py`, `out/scorecard.html`).

**Promises.** There is a scorecard, and it is about FPL. One second to parse, zero
to explain.

**Evidence.** `fplscorecard.com` **FREE**, `.co.uk` **FREE**, `.io` **FREE**, `.app`
**FREE** — a clean sweep, the only candidate here with one. X `@fplscorecard`
**free** (404). Reddit inconclusive.

**Strongest argument against.** You cannot own it. It is a generic abbreviation plus
a descriptive noun; registering it as a mark in Class 41/42 is a long shot, and the
day this works someone launches "The FPL Scorecard" or "FPL Score Card" and you have
no remedy. You also inherit every weakness of the convention — invisible in a field
of eleven near-identical names, and locked to FPL forever, so a Champions League or
Serie A product later means a full rename with no brand equity carried over.
Tertiary: "scorecard" is cricket and golf vocabulary before it is proof vocabulary,
so the honesty claim does not actually survive in the word.

### 3. Kickoff Ledger

**Means.** A ledger is append-only, timestamped and auditable; "kickoff" locates it
in football and in the moment *before* the result. It maps literally onto the
mechanism — each projection is a commit pushed to GitHub before the deadline.

**Promises.** Entries go in before kickoff and cannot be edited after.

**Evidence.** `kickoffledger.com` **FREE**, `.io` **FREE**, `.app` **FREE**.
`kickoffledger.co.uk` **TAKEN** — registered 2026-08-10 via Hosting Concepts B.V.
(Openprovider), with the Nominet note "was not able to match the registrant's name
and/or address against a 3rd party source", which reads like a reseller or squatter
holding rather than a trading business. X `@kickoffledger` **free** (404).

**Strongest argument against.** "Ledger" now means crypto to most people, and this
product already says "cryptographically snapshotted" — the two together make a
football audience read it as a coin-adjacent thing, which is a credibility
liability in exactly the community you need to win over. Ledger SAS is also a famous
mark, which is a headache you do not need. And for a UK-first business the missing
piece is `.co.uk`, which is the wrong TLD of the four to lose.

### 4. Ante (as *Ante FPL* / `antefpl`)

**Means.** *Ex ante* — the technical term, used throughout the README, for a
prediction made before the event. The poker sense is the same idea: what you put in
before you see the cards.

**Promises.** Everything here was said in advance.

**Evidence.** `antefpl.com` **FREE**, `.co.uk` **FREE**, `.io` **FREE**, `.app`
**FREE**. X `@antefpl` **free** (404). (Bare `exante` is closed on every TLD:
`.com` 1996, `.co.uk` 2012, `.io` 2019, `.app` taken — EXANTE is an established
brokerage, and that is a Class 36 financial-services mark you do not want near a
subscription product.)

**Strongest argument against.** The poker sense is louder than the Latin one, and a
fantasy sports product that sounds like gambling is a genuine commercial problem in
the UK — payment processors, app store categorisation, advertising restrictions,
and a Premier League that is currently distancing itself from betting sponsorship.
Say-out-loud is also weaker than it looks: "ANT-ee" vs "AHN-tay" splits listeners,
and "ante" gets typed as "anti" by a meaningful share of people.

### 5. Predeadline

**Means.** The literal fact the product is built on. Every scored projection was
recorded before the deadline; the horizon bands (T−72h, T−24h, T−6h, T−2h) are the
product's actual structure.

**Promises.** Timestamped before the gate closed.

**Evidence.** `predeadline.com` **FREE**, `.co.uk` **FREE**, `.io` **FREE**, `.app`
**FREE**. X `@predeadline` **free** (404).

**Strongest argument against.** It is a modifier, not a name — "pre-deadline" is an
adjective people already use in this exact context ("my pre-deadline team"), so the
brand will be permanently swallowed by ordinary sentences and is close to
unsearchable and close to unregistrable. It also fails the hyphen test: spoken
once, it is written back as "pre-deadline", "predeadline" and "Pre Deadline" in
roughly equal thirds, and you will be correcting URLs forever.

### 6. FPL Commit

**Means.** Double sense: to commit to a prediction, and the git commit that *is* the
external timestamp (README: "The push is the external timestamp").

**Promises.** We commit, in public, before we know.

**Evidence.** `fplcommit.com` **FREE**, `.co.uk` **FREE**, `.io` **FREE**, `.app`
**FREE**. X `@fplcommit` **free** (404).

**Strongest argument against.** The good half of the meaning is invisible to the
audience — the git sense lands only with developers, and FPL's buying public is not
developers. What is left is a verb with no object, which reads as unfinished
("commit to what?"). "Commit" also carries an unhelpful ambient sense of obligation
and lock-in for something you want people to subscribe to.

### 7. Brier (as *Brier FPL* / `brierfpl`)

**Means.** The Brier score — the proper scoring rule for probabilistic forecasts,
and the metric the README actually reports for the minutes model (0.0817
out-of-sample vs 0.1014 heuristic).

**Promises.** We are a forecasting shop that grades itself on a real rule.

**Evidence.** Bare `brier` is fully closed: `.com` 1998, `.co.uk` 2021, `.io` 2018,
`.app` taken. `brierscore.com` **TAKEN** (2026-01-01, currently a "Brier Score
Calculator"), `brierscore.co.uk` / `.io` / `.app` **FREE**. X `@BrierScore`
**TAKEN** (a personal account, "Caio"). `brierfpl.com` / `.co.uk` / `.io` / `.app`
all **FREE**; X `@brierfpl` **free** (404).

**Strongest argument against.** It fails the say-it-out-loud test badly. Heard on a
podcast, "Brier" is written back as *Briar*, *Bryer* or *Breyer* by most listeners,
and you cannot defend against that by buying variants of a name you already had to
suffix with "FPL" because the clean version was gone. And the reference is opaque:
to the 98% who have not met a scoring rule, it is a shrub or a surname, so it
carries none of the positioning — it only signals to people who were going to
believe you anyway.

### 8. FPL Receipts — **eliminated, listed so it isn't re-proposed**

**Means.** "We have the receipts." The best vernacular fit for the positioning that
exists, and the phrase FPL X actually uses.

**Evidence.** `fplreceipts.com` **TAKEN**, created 2026-07-26, on Vercel DNS,
currently serving a live product: title *"FPL Receipts — FPL Squad, Gameweek & Chip
Planner 2026/27"*. X `@FplReceipts` **TAKEN** by an account named "FPLReceipts".

**Strongest argument against.** It is taken, by a same-category FPL tool, launched
three weeks ago and actively building an audience. Direct collision. Dead. Worth
knowing that the obvious vernacular name went in July 2026 — the good names in this
category are being taken right now, which is an argument for deciding this week.

## 5. Recommendation

### Take **Proper Score**.

Reasoning, in order of weight:

1. **It is the only candidate whose meaning *is* the business model.** The product's
   claim is not "we are accurate", it is "we are graded by something we cannot rig,
   and we publish the losses." A proper scoring rule is the mathematical name for
   that exact property. Every other candidate names a *feature* of the mechanism —
   the timestamp, the ledger, the commit — and features get copied. The ethic does
   not.
2. **It survives contact with a microphone.** Two common English words, four
   syllables, one spelling, no hyphen, no ambiguity. Someone hears "Proper Score" on
   a podcast and types it correctly on the first try. Measured against Brier
   (three spellings), Predeadline (three spellings) and Ante (two pronunciations),
   this is the whole ballgame — the README is right that the moat is audience, not
   accuracy, and a name with a spelling tax is a leak in the only moat you have.
3. **It is registrable.** "Proper Score" for fantasy football statistical services is
   suggestive rather than descriptive — you have a real shot at a Class 41/42 mark,
   which none of the FPL-prefix candidates offer. That matters specifically because
   the accuracy record is the asset, and an asset that anyone can put their name on
   is not an asset.
4. **It is not a Premier League problem.** No PL term, no implied endorsement, no
   confusion with the official game or with "The Scout". Pair it with the standard
   non-affiliation footer and the legal surface is about as small as it gets.
5. **It has somewhere to go.** If this ever becomes a Champions League product, a
   Serie A product, or a general forecasting brand, the name comes along. "FPL
   Scorecard" does not.

**Buy `properscore.co.uk` as primary** (correct for a UK-audience product and the
TLD both incumbents use — `fantasyfootballscout.co.uk`, `fantasyfootballhub.co.uk`),
**plus `.io` and `.app` defensively**, and make a low, unemotional offer for
`properscore.com`, which has sat dormant since 2015. Do not let the `.com` block the
decision — Fantasy Football Scout has built a £30/yr business on a `.co.uk` for
fifteen years.

**Live with the objection.** The Spearman-is-not-a-proper-scoring-rule nitpick is
real, and the answer is to make it a feature rather than hide from it: report RMSE
and Brier alongside Spearman on the scorecard — the code already computes all three
— and put a one-paragraph "why we're called this" note on the About page that
states the definition and admits Spearman is a rank statistic reported for
interpretability. A brand built on publishing your losses can afford to publish that
one too. It is the most on-message possible response.

### Runner-up: **FPL Scorecard**

If the abstraction feels like a luxury the product cannot afford yet — a defensible
position when there are zero subscribers and zero scored gameweeks — take
`FPL Scorecard`. It is the only candidate with all four TLDs and the X handle free,
it explains itself in one second, and "scorecard" is already the word the codebase
and the site use, so nothing has to be re-taught. You are trading away legal
defensibility and every future expansion path for immediate legibility. Go in with
open eyes: that trade cannot be reversed, and someone will eventually launch a name
one character away from yours.

**Do not** take Kickoff Ledger, Brier, Predeadline, Ante or FPL Commit. Each fails a
different test that matters more than the thing it is good at.

## 6. What this implies for the logo

**Proper Score wants a wordmark. Not a monogram, not a symbol.**

- **Wordmark, because the brand asset is a claim, and symbols cannot make claims.**
  The entire proposition is a sentence ("graded by a rule you cannot game"). An
  abstract mark would have to be taught what it means, which is precisely the cost
  the naming strategy is trying to avoid paying twice.
- **A monogram is unavailable.** "PS" reads as postscript or PlayStation. Do not
  attempt it.
- **Register: laboratory, not sportsbook.** A plain, slightly unfashionable
  grotesque — the typographic voice of a test report. The site's copy already sits
  in this register ("a baseline anyone could build in an afternoon", "including the
  weeks we lose"); the logo should not be louder than the writing. Set both words at
  the same weight — do not emphasise "Proper", which turns a technical term into a
  boast.
- **If a mark is wanted at all, there is exactly one honest option:** a minimal
  two-bar glyph — us against the baseline — that can render *either way round*. It
  is a mark that shows a loss as readily as a win, which is the only symbol
  consistent with the product. A tick, a trophy, an upward arrow or a crown all
  assert the thing the brand refuses to assert.
- **Hard exclusions:** no football, no shield, no crown, no lion, no jersey number,
  no stadium silhouette. And **nothing in the region of Premier League purple
  (#37003C)** — colour proximity plus a fantasy football product is how an
  implied-endorsement argument gets built.
- **Lockup:** wordmark over a fixed descriptor line, `FPL projections, on the
  record`, at roughly a third the size. The descriptor is not decoration — it is the
  legibility half of the naming strategy, and it ships everywhere the wordmark does
  until the name is known.

*(If the runner-up is chosen instead: "FPL Scorecard" also wants a wordmark, with
"FPL" set lighter and "Scorecard" heavier so the differentiating word carries the
weight. Same exclusions apply, and doubly so — a descriptive name next to
league-adjacent visual cues is the worst combination for implied endorsement.)*
